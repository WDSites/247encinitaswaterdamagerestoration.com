Condo Insurance and Water Damage in Encinitas, CA

Condo water damage usually involves more than one policy. Knowing which one to read first keeps the claim moving.

By the 247 Encinitas Water Damage Restoration team. Published October 26, 2026.

☎ (760) 769-1159

Two policies, one wet unit

Owning a condo means owning a slice of a shared building, and insurance follows that split. Your homeowners policy for condos, often called an HO-6, usually covers what is inside your unit, such as personal property and sometimes interior finishes. The association's master policy usually covers the building structure and common areas.

Where one ends and the other begins varies by association and by policy. Some master policies cover the walls out to bare drywall, others cover fixtures and cabinets, and many leave interior finishes to the owner. The only reliable way to know is to read your policy and the governing documents of your association.

Condo leaks also behave differently from single-family leaks. Water travels through shared walls and floors, so one failed supply line can reach three units, and speed in the first day often matters more than who is responsible.

When the leak starts in the unit above

This is the case that causes the most arguments. A burst line, an overflowing tub or a failed water heater upstairs can soak your ceiling, walls and floor in minutes. Who pays depends on the cause, the documents and the policies on both sides.

As a general matter, your own policy is often where a claim for your unit starts, and your insurer may then pursue the responsible party. In other cases the association or the upstairs owner's liability coverage comes into play. Read your policy and governing documents first, then put the question to your insurer before repairs are scheduled.

Do not wait on the paperwork

Most insurers want the property kept from getting worse while a claim is open. Stopping water, drying the unit and documenting the loss can happen while the coverage question is still open, and keeping receipts for that work helps later.

Deductibles and loss assessment, in plain words

Two terms come up in almost every condo claim. A deductible is the amount you pay before coverage applies, and a master policy may carry a large one that the association passes on to the owners involved. Loss assessment coverage on an HO-6 can help with that kind of charge in some policies.

Ask your agent whether your policy includes loss assessment, what the limit is, and whether it applies to a deductible charged by the association. A short phone call before a loss costs nothing and can save an unpleasant surprise later.

A ground-floor unit and the flood exclusion

Rising water from rain or runoff is treated differently from a pipe that bursts. As the California Department of Insurance puts it, homeowners and commercial policies typically exclude flood, mudslide and debris flow, and that flood coverage comes from the federal National Flood Insurance Program, which the department does not regulate.

A new flood policy typically takes effect 30 days after purchase, so it will not help after a storm has already started. NFIP flood claims can be reported at 1-800-638-6620. Ask your insurer which of your coverages apply to storm water before you assume anything.

Documentation that helps the claim

  • Dated photos and short videos of every wet room, wide and close, before anything moves.
  • A short written timeline of when the leak started, who you contacted and what was said.
  • Names and policy numbers for your insurer, the association and management.
  • Receipts for water extraction, drying, hotel stays and temporary repairs.
  • Photos of the source, such as a failed fitting or a stained ceiling in the unit above.

Our water damage cleanup team documents moisture readings, affected materials and the work performed, and we leave a written record you can pass to your insurer. We do not decide coverage, and we make no promises about what a claim will pay.

Encinitas local note

Encinitas note: flood coverage is separate

Lagoons and creeks shape flood risk along this stretch of coast, and the department is direct that standard homeowners and commercial policies typically exclude flood. Flood insurance is the federal National Flood Insurance Program.

Practically, ask your agent and your association whether any flood coverage exists for your building, and remember that a new NFIP policy typically waits 30 days to take effect. That department adds that the NFIP defines a flood as inundation of two or more acres of normally dry land or of two or more properties, one of them yours.
